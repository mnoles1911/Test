# Mira-Thal Lore Bible

World reference for the **Mira-Thal Trilogy** — a three-game medieval low-fantasy RPG.

## Where to start

**`INDEX.md`** is the directory map. It lists every lore file by category:
world & reference, locations (35+), characters, backstories, organizations,
plot (game by game, including the per-knowledge-beat plot spine in
`GAME1_KNOWLEDGE_PLOT.md`), level layouts (Acts I–IV, scene by scene), and
histories. Use it as the table of contents — this README is just orientation.

If you only have a minute, read `WORLD.md` and `REFERENCE.md`.

## World at a Glance

**World:** Mira-Thal — two continents. Mira (civilized west), Thal (wild east).

**Era:** The Long Twilight — the Third Age, the Age of Men. Humanity is
ascending. The world's deeper fabric is fraying.

**Protagonist:** Roland Ashford — former Iron Chalice knight, expelled for
refusing silence about a relic that turned out to be a piece of the Sundered
Crown.

**Antagonist:** Mordvar (born Bealoric). A Second Age human king who emptied
himself to escape death, became immortal and empty, and was unmade two thousand
years ago at the Sorrowmarsh. Mordvar is reforming.

**Companions across the trilogy:** Corvus Tane (mage), Seren of the Third Glade
(Aelorin), Orion Farr (sailor), Dagna Irontrack (Dragon-Watcher), Aldric Vane
(smith, last of the Caelborn line), Edran Vane (investigator). Full profiles in
`CHARACTERS_COMPANIONS.md`.

**The Trilogy Arc:**
- **Game One — The Sundered Crown:** Reassemble the seven-piece binding
  artifact to destroy Mordvar's regent and shatter his command structure.
- **Game Two — The Singing Vault:** Descend into the dwarven hold of Khorumzad
  to recover the Aeluvain — the fallen Eighth Star, the only thing that can
  truly end Mordvar.
- **Game Three — The Last Star:** Find Aldric Vane, the last of the Caelborn
  line (Aescryd-blooded), the only person whose lineage allows him to wield the
  Aeluvain against Mordvar. Fight the Battle of Drûn-Khazad.

## Tone and Themes

Low fantasy. No chosen ones, no prophecies, no divine intervention. The heroes
are people doing the hardest available thing because no one else will. The world
has beauty and rot in equal measure. Victories are earned and cost something.
The enemy is not evil in the abstract — Mordvar was a man who made a terrible
choice out of ordinary fear, and understanding this does not make him less
dangerous.

The governing theme: **bonds between people are more durable than institutions,
and more powerful than any single act of heroism.**
